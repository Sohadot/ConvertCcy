Mexico:
Foreign Currency Rules

Currency: MXN — Mexican Peso ISO: MX / MEX Last reviewed: 2026-09-06 Lifecycle: published Indexing: allowed

Country Overview

Mexico operates a free-floating exchange-rate regime, set by the Comisión de Cambios — officials of the Secretaría de Hacienda y Crédito Público (SHCP) and Banco de México — under Article 21 of the Ley del Banco de México, with Banco de México as the central bank. Under Article 8 of the Ley Monetaria, foreign currency is not legal tender in Mexico and payment obligations denominated in foreign currency for fulfilment in Mexico are discharged in pesos at the prevailing exchange rate. Cross-border movements of cash by travellers are administered by the customs authorities under Article 9 of the Ley Aduanera, which requires a mandatory declaration when means of payment exceed the equivalent of USD 10,000. Banks separately apply anti-money-laundering controls, together with quantitative limits on US-dollar cash operations for individual users differentiated by nationality, under Article 115 of the Ley de Instituciones de Crédito — anti-money-laundering and cash-operation-limit measures, not an exchange control. The reviewed official sources do not establish a general Banco de México authorisation or approval requirement for holding or transferring currency; this reflects the limits of the sources reviewed, not a positive finding that no sectoral or capital-movement restriction exists. The customs, exchange-rate, AML and reporting regimes are kept distinct and are not conflated.

Rules at a Glance

Bringing foreign currency into the country
A traveller entering Mexico must file a mandatory customs declaration when the means of payment carried — cash, national or foreign checks, payment orders or any other collectible document, or a combination — exceed the equivalent of USD 10,000 in total (Ley Aduanera, Article 9), on the official form approved by the SAT. At or below that amount no declaration is required. The operational customs authority is the Agencia Nacional de Aduanas de México (ANAM). This customs declaration is distinct from the anti-money-laundering controls and cash-operation limits on US-dollar cash operations at banks.
Taking foreign currency out of the country
A traveller leaving Mexico must likewise file a mandatory customs declaration when the means of payment carried exceed the equivalent of USD 10,000 in total (Ley Aduanera, Article 9); the obligation is symmetric with entry. The reviewed official sources do not establish a Banco de México permission or export limit on physically carrying currency out of the country; the only monetary threshold on the traveller identified is the customs declaration duty. Anti-money-laundering controls and US-dollar cash-operation limits apply separately to cash operations conducted through banks.
Cash declaration threshold
The customs declaration threshold is means of payment — cash, national or foreign checks, payment orders or any other collectible document, or a combination — exceeding the equivalent of USD 10,000 in total, for a traveller entering or leaving the country (Ley Aduanera, Article 9). It is a mandatory report to the customs authorities (ANAM) on the SAT-approved form, triggered only above USD 10,000; failure to declare carries a fine of 20% to 40% of the excess (Article 185, fracción VII). This border declaration is separate from three other regimes: anti-money-laundering controls and quantitative limits on US-dollar cash operations (Ley de Instituciones de Crédito, Article 115), UIF suspicious- and cash-transaction reporting, and the LFPIORPI domestic cash-payment limits.
Rules for residents holding foreign currency
Under Banco de México Circular 3/2012, credit institutions may open peso (national-currency) deposit accounts to both natural and legal persons, with the lowest-tier and special-savings accounts reserved for natural persons. The reviewed official sources do not establish an unrestricted right for any resident to hold a foreign-currency (US-dollar) account: foreign-currency sight deposits payable in Mexico are available only to defined categories — natural persons domiciled in the northern 20-kilometre border strip or in Baja California and Baja California Sur, legal persons domiciled in national territory, and official representations of foreign governments and international organisations — while US-dollar transfer (SPID) accounts are opened only to legal persons domiciled in national territory. Banks also apply anti-money-laundering monitoring and reporting requirements to foreign-currency cash operations, together with quantitative daily and monthly limits on US-dollar cash operations for individual users differentiated by nationality, under the general provisions for Article 115 of the Ley de Instituciones de Crédito. Account opening remains subject to AML/KYC and each institution's own conditions; eligibility does not guarantee that any particular account will be opened.
Rules for non-residents
The reviewed official sources do not establish a general entitlement for non-residents to hold foreign-currency accounts in Mexico: eligibility for foreign-currency sight deposits and for US-dollar transfer (SPID) accounts is tied to domicile in national territory, so non-resident persons generally fall outside these categories, aside from official representations of foreign governments and international organisations. A separate peso-denominated deposit and prepaid-card regime exists for Mexican nationals whose place of residence is abroad. US-dollar cash operations are additionally subject to anti-money-laundering controls and to quantitative limits for individual users differentiated by nationality under Article 115 of the Ley de Instituciones de Crédito. Account opening remains subject to sanctions, anti-money-laundering and know-your-customer screening and each institution's requirements; there is no entitlement to any particular account or service.
Business invoicing and settlement in foreign currency
Under Article 8 of the Ley Monetaria, obligations payable in Mexico that are denominated in foreign currency are discharged by delivering the peso equivalent at the exchange rate prevailing on the payment date (foreign currency is not legal tender), subject to the statute's exception for payments originating in funds transferred from abroad. Cross-border business payments and receipts run through banks and other intermediaries authorised under Mexican law; the reviewed official sources do not establish a general Banco de México authorisation requirement for ordinary cross-border payments. Anti-money-laundering controls and US-dollar cash-operation limits (Ley de Instituciones de Crédito, Article 115) and reporting obligations apply. This does not establish an unrestricted choice of invoice currency, which depends on the contract and the Ley Monetaria settlement rule.
Exchange controls and approval requirements
Mexico's foreign-exchange regime is free-floating and set by the Comisión de Cambios — the SHCP and Banco de México — under Article 21 of the Ley del Banco de México; the Comisión determines the exchange-rate regime and Banco de México sets the rules for determining the exchange rate (Ley Monetaria, Article 8). The reviewed official sources do not establish a general authorisation, permission or approval regime for buying, holding or transferring currency; this reflects the limits of the sources reviewed, not a positive finding that no sectoral or capital-movement restriction exists. The operative restrictions identified are of a different character and are kept distinct — anti-money-laundering controls and quantitative limits on US-dollar cash operations (Ley de Instituciones de Crédito, Article 115), the customs border declaration (Ley Aduanera, Article 9), and AML reporting. Whether any narrower sectoral or capital-movement restriction exists outside the reviewed sources is not established either way.
Banking and conversion practicality
Currency conversion and cross-border payments in Mexico are provided through banks, and through casas de cambio and centros cambiarios authorised and supervised under Mexican law by the Comisión Nacional Bancaria y de Valores (CNBV) and Banco de México. These authorised intermediaries apply anti-money-laundering identification and controls on US-dollar cash operations under Article 115 of the Ley de Instituciones de Crédito, including quantitative limits on US-dollar cash operations for individual users differentiated by nationality; authorisation does not guarantee that any particular institution offers a given currency, service or price.

Practical Summaries

Traveler summary

Travellers entering or leaving Mexico must file a mandatory customs declaration when the cash, national or foreign checks, payment orders or other collectible instruments they carry — or a combination — exceed the equivalent of USD 10,000 in total (Ley Aduanera, Article 9). The duty is symmetric on entry and exit and is filed on the official form approved by the SAT; failure to declare carries a fine of 20% to 40% of the amount exceeding USD 10,000 (Article 185, fracción VII). This customs declaration is separate from the anti-money-laundering controls and cash-operation limits banks apply to US-dollar cash operations.

Business summary

Under Article 8 of the Ley Monetaria, obligations payable in Mexico that are denominated in foreign currency are discharged in pesos at the exchange rate prevailing on the payment date; foreign currency is not legal tender. Currency conversion and cross-border payments run through banks and other intermediaries authorised under Mexican law, and the exchange-rate regime is free-floating and set by the Comisión de Cambios. The reviewed official sources do not establish a general Banco de México authorisation or approval requirement for making cross-border payments or transferring funds — the limits of the sources reviewed, not a positive finding that no restriction exists. Banks separately apply anti-money-laundering controls and quantitative US-dollar cash-operation limits under Article 115 of the Ley de Instituciones de Crédito.
