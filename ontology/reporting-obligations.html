Currency Passage Class 5

Reporting Obligations

Who reports (individuals, banks, carriers) and to whom. The compliance layer that exists beyond declaration. Multiple parties may file reports on the same transaction, creating audit trails independent of traveler behavior.

Definition

The Reporting Chain

Reporting obligations create multiple touch points in the compliance system. The chain can include:

  • Individual reporting. The traveler files a declaration (customs form, bank form). This is traveler-initiated.
  • Bank reporting. Financial institutions file reports (Currency Transaction Reports, Suspicious Activity Reports) for transactions above thresholds or exhibiting unusual patterns. This is automatic, not traveler-initiated.
  • Customs reporting. Border officials file reports of declared amounts and patterns. This is authority-initiated.
  • Reporting recipient. Where do reports go? Central bank? FIU (Financial Intelligence Unit)? Tax authority? AML regulator? The recipient determines follow-up consequences.
  • Retention period. How long are reports kept? How long are they shared? Can individuals access their own reports?
Regulatory Significance

Reporting obligations are the intelligence layer. They exist even when declaration is not required or when the traveler successfully declares. Banks, customs, and carriers are witness mechanisms independent of compliance intent.

Real-World Examples

How Reporting Chains Operate

United States
Dual Reporting: Traveler + Bank + Customs
Travelers declaring USD 10,000+ file FinCEN Form 105 at customs (traveler-initiated). Banks file Currency Transaction Reports (CTRs) for any transaction USD 10,000+ (bank-initiated, automatic, daily aggregation). If a pattern of structuring is detected (multiple transactions just under the threshold), Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) are filed to FinCEN. All reports flow to FinCEN, which shares with Secret Service, DEA, and state AML authorities. Retention: 5 years minimum.
  • Three reporting pathways: traveler declaration, bank CTR, bank SAR
  • Reports go to FinCEN, then distributed to law enforcement and regulators
  • Individuals cannot easily access their own reports

India
Customs + Bank Reporting, Limited Traveler Initiation
Travelers declare on customs form D if carrying foreign currency above threshold. Banks file Currency Transaction Reports (CTR) to FISC (Financial Intelligence Security Council) for conversions above INR 50,000 (USD ~600). Large transactions or patterns also trigger Suspicious Transaction Reports (STR). Customs officers file separate reports on declared amounts. All reports flow to the FIU (Financial Intelligence Unit), which shares with tax authorities and enforcement. Reporting is real-time; retention is permanent.
  • Threshold low (INR 50,000); reporting very frequent
  • Bank reporting is automatic and mandatory
  • Customs and bank reports are separate; data may cross-check

European Union
Harmonized EU FIU Reporting
Travelers declare at Customs (Schengen borders) if carrying EUR 10,000+. Banks file Suspicious Transaction Reports (STR) to national FIUs for patterns of activity, regardless of transaction size. Customs files border reports to national FIUs. All national FIUs share data through Egmont Group and EU FIU network. Retention: 5 years. Individuals can request their own reports through GDPR subject access.
  • Harmonized threshold (EUR 10,000)
  • Focus on pattern detection (STR) rather than transaction volume reporting
  • GDPR gives individuals transparency into their own reports

China
Centralized Bank + SAFE Reporting
Individuals converting foreign currency through banks report through bank processes (documentation required for permitted use). Banks file reports on all conversions above certain thresholds (often all conversions >USD 5,000) to SAFE (State Administration of Foreign Exchange). Customs also files separate reports on currency imports/exports. All reports go to SAFE, which integrates with tax authority and state security. Retention: Permanent; individual access is restricted.
  • Single authority (SAFE) aggregates all reporting
  • Reporting thresholds are lower; coverage is comprehensive
  • Individual access to reports is limited

Design Decisions

Why Reporting Systems Vary

Threshold Sensitivity

High threshold (EUR 10,000, USD 10,000): fewer reports, focus on larger transactions. Low threshold (INR 50,000 ≈ USD 600): more reports, mass surveillance capability. The threshold reflects risk tolerance and administrative capacity.

Reporting Locus

Bank-primary reporting (EU, US): spreads burden to financial institutions; creates decentralized visibility. Authority-primary reporting (China, some emerging markets): centralizes intelligence in one regulator; simpler but requires state capacity.

Transparency Model

Transparent reporting (EU under GDPR): individuals can access their own reports, creating accountability. Opaque reporting (China, some emerging markets): individuals cannot access; reports are state intelligence tools. The choice reflects trust in government vs individual rights.

For Passage Planning

Know the reporting chain: (1) Will your transaction trigger a report? (2) Which authority will file it? (3) Where does it go? (4) Can you access it? Understanding the reporting layer is part of compliance, separate from declaration.

Related Classes

How Reporting Connects

Reporting obligations are the enforcement mechanism for other classes:

  • Declaration Regimes (Class 1): Reports verify whether declarations occurred and are accurate.
  • Exchange Controls (Class 2): Bank reports flag conversions that violate control limits or licensing.
  • Penalty Regimes (Class 7): Reports trigger investigations that lead to penalties.
  • Channel Restrictions (Class 6): Bank reporting enforces channel-specific rules (off-channel conversions detected through reporting gaps).
