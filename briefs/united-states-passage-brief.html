United States — Currency Passage Brief

USD · United States Dollar · North America

For travellers

Carrying cash in or out

Travelers may carry unlimited US dollars or foreign currency, but any person transporting more than USD 10,000 in aggregate — cash or monetary instruments — into or out of the United States must file a Currency and Monetary Instrument Report (FinCEN Form 105) with CBP at the border. Amounts of USD 10,000 or less require no declaration.

  • Bringing currency in: Foreign currency and US dollars may be imported without limit or prohibition. Any person bringing more than USD 10,000 in aggregate (cash or monetary instruments) into the United States must file FinCEN Form 105 (CMIR) with a CBP officer at the port of entry.
  • Taking currency out: US dollars and foreign currency may be physically transported out without a maximum amount, but transporting more than USD 10,000 in aggregate currency or covered monetary instruments requires FinCEN Form 105.
  • Declaration: The reporting threshold is USD 10,000. Under 31 U.S.C. 5316 and 31 CFR 1010.340, transporting an aggregate amount exceeding that figure across the US border triggers a mandatory Currency and Monetary Instrument Report (FinCEN Form 105) to CBP. This traveler border report is distinct from Bank Secrecy Act filings by financial institutions — Currency Transaction Reports (CTR) and Suspicious Activity Reports (SAR) — and from Foreign Bank Account Reports (FBAR); none of those is a traveler declaration.
Residency

Resident vs non-resident

  • Residents: The United States does not maintain a general exchange-control regime over residents' foreign-currency holdings: under the OECD Code of Liberalisation of Capital Movements, the US reservation schedule contains no general capital-movement reservation restricting residents' holding, conversion, transfer, or repatriation of foreign currency. Its reservations are limited to enumerated sectoral inward direct investment and a small-issues securities-registration carve-out for non-resident issuers. Account access remains subject to sanctions, AML/KYC, tax, information-reporting, and prudential requirements.
  • Non-residents: For non-residents, the US schedule under the OECD Code of Liberalisation of Capital Movements contains no general reservation restricting ordinary transfer or repatriation; the recorded reservations concern enumerated inward-investment sectors and limited securities-registration treatment for non-resident issuers. This does not entitle every non-resident to every bank account or service: onboarding remains subject to sanctions screening, AML/KYC, information-reporting and withholding, and each institution's requirements.
For business

Invoicing, settlement & conversion

For current international transactions, businesses and individuals can make and receive payments and transfers without exchange restrictions under the United States' IMF Article VIII status, except for measures maintained solely for security reasons. Foreign-currency account access, repatriation, and capital-account movements are governed separately and remain an open evidence gap pending the US OECD reservation schedule.

  • Invoicing & settlement: For current international transactions, payments and transfers are not subject to general exchange restrictions under the United States' IMF Article VIII status, except for measures maintained for security reasons. This finding does not establish rules on invoice denomination or capital-account transactions.
  • Exchange controls: The United States has a free-floating exchange-rate arrangement and, having accepted IMF Article VIII, maintains an exchange system free of restrictions on payments and transfers for current international transactions, with no multiple currency practices — except for restrictions maintained solely for the preservation of national or international security, notified to the IMF. This finding covers current international transactions; capital-account movements are governed separately under the OECD Code of Liberalisation of Capital Movements and the US reservation schedule.
  • Banking & conversion: Ordinary currency conversion and cross-border payments operate through regulated US financial institutions and money-services businesses, subject to BSA/AML, sanctions, tax, reporting, and institution-specific controls. Currency dealers or exchangers and money transmitters are money services businesses that must register with FinCEN under the Bank Secrecy Act and maintain applicable compliance programs.
